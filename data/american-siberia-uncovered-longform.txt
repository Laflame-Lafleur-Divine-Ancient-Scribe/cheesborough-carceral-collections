The American Siberia Uncovered
J. C. Powell, Florida Convict Leasing, and the People Who Lived Inside the Camps

Article by Jehlani C.

In the fall of 1876, a train arrived in Live Oak carrying a group of Florida prisoners whose appearance J. C. Powell never forgot. He remembered roughly thirty men coming off the train in chains after an earlier period of railroad labor near Lake Eustis, their clothing deteriorated and their bodies weakened by sickness, hunger, exposure, and neglect. For Powell, their arrival marked the beginning of a fourteen year encounter with a prison system that often existed far beyond the walls of a conventional penitentiary.

The Florida Powell entered was still rebuilding itself after the Civil War and Reconstruction. The state had only recently attempted to organize a permanent penitentiary at Chattahoochee, and by the late 1870s officials were increasingly turning toward arrangements in which incarcerated people could be placed under private contractors and moved wherever their labor was needed. Railroad grades, agricultural operations, brickyards, forests, and turpentine camps became extensions of state custody. The prison could therefore move with the work.

Powell published his recollections in 1891 under the remarkable title The American Siberia, or Fourteen Years' Experience in a Southern Convict Camp. The title page identified him as "Captain of the Florida Convict Camp," making clear that he was not a visitor describing a strange institution from the outside. He had participated in its administration, supervised incarcerated workers, dealt with guards and contractors, pursued people who escaped, enforced discipline, and watched the lease system develop around him.

That background is independently important because the federal census brings Powell out of his own memoir and places him inside an administrative record. The 1880 census transcription preserved with this project records John C. Powell, age twenty nine, as "Captain of guard." Directly associated with him is Henry A. Wyse, identified as "Contractor-State Prison," along with captains, commissary workers, and a large group of named guards. Powell's memoir does not have to prove that he belonged to Florida's prison system because the census already places him there.

What makes the book even more compelling is that Powell was not the only person who crossed from memory into the archive. Names that appear in his stories begin reappearing in census schedules and prison registers. Some matches are unusually strong, some remain possible, and others resist identification altogether. The result is not a simple exercise in proving Powell right or wrong. It is a reconstruction of a world in which memory and government records sometimes agree and sometimes preserve very different endings.

Florida Before Powell

Powell inherited a prison system that had already been changing for years. Florida's first sustained experiment with centralized imprisonment took shape at Chattahoochee, where the state converted a former federal arsenal into a penitentiary after the Civil War. Malachi Martin became commanding officer in 1868 and later continued as warden after Florida reorganized the prison under civilian administration.

Martin's career shows how closely early prison administration was connected to Reconstruction government. He had served as a Union Army quartermaster before settling in Florida, and his later public life included agriculture, prison management, Republican politics, and service as Speaker of the Florida House in 1874. Powell later described Martin in severe terms, but later archival research complicates several portions of Powell's account, including his chronology of Martin's arrival in Florida and the extent of the fortune Powell claimed Martin accumulated through prison labor.

The documented record nevertheless shows that labor stood at the center of Chattahoochee. Martin reported agricultural production, construction, fencing, brickmaking, and other work carried out by prisoners for the benefit of the institution and the state. Later investigators also concluded that incarcerated labor had been used on Martin's private agricultural property and vineyard, illustrating how easily the boundaries between public custody and private benefit could become blurred.

By the time Powell entered the system in the 1870s, Florida was moving still farther from the idea of a single prison concentrated at Chattahoochee. Custody increasingly followed contracts, transportation routes, and private enterprise. The people Powell supervised could be housed in temporary camps, marched into forests, attached to work squads, or assigned to industries located many miles from anything resembling a permanent state institution.

When a Forest Became a Prison

Powell's description of Camp Padlock shows how little architecture was necessary to create confinement. He remembered a rough log sleeping house approximately twenty feet by forty feet, built in the woods and lacking the familiar walls and cell blocks of a permanent prison. Inside, prisoners slept on platforms while chains connected them to the building, and guards could watch through the slatted front of the structure.

Powell described several forms of restraint. Leg irons limited movement, waist chains secured individuals, and a larger building chain tied the sleeping population to the structure at night. When men traveled together, a squad chain could keep several people connected while they moved between camp and work. The system depended less on masonry than on iron, armed guards, geography, and the knowledge that escape could bring an organized pursuit.

Even the daily routine reveals how portable the institution had become. Powell remembered simple meals of bacon, cornbread, and cowpeas prepared under rough outdoor conditions. Guards conducted evening chain inspections, an armed watchman remained outside, and prisoners needed permission to move after the camp settled for the night.

The landscape completed what the chains began. Pine forests, rivers, swamps, heat, insects, distance from towns, and unfamiliar country made leaving a camp dangerous even when a person managed to get beyond the guards. Powell's memoir repeatedly returns to escape because the geography of Florida became part of both imprisonment and resistance.

Pine, Resin, and the Turpentine Chore

Few sections of The American Siberia are as useful for understanding daily labor as Powell's descriptions of turpentine work. The industry depended on the resin of Florida's pine trees, which could be distilled into spirits of turpentine while the heavier material left behind became rosin. These products belonged to the naval stores trade, an important part of the southern forest economy.

The work began at the tree. Workers cut collecting cavities near the base of pine trunks and opened the surface above them so that resin could move downward. As gum accumulated, it was removed with dipping tools, transferred into containers, placed into barrels, hauled to a still, and distilled. Fresh cuts had to be made repeatedly so that the tree would continue producing resin.

Powell describes chipping as particularly demanding. The worker moved from pine to pine carrying a specialized cutting tool, repeatedly striking the tree to expose fresh wood while trying to maintain the pace expected by the camp. As the worked section of the trunk rose higher, longer handled tools allowed the cuts to continue above ordinary reach.

For an incarcerated worker, this was not simply agricultural or industrial employment. The same person who controlled the pace of work could also participate in discipline, restraint, or pursuit. Powell remembered squads moving quickly through the woods and described exhausted prisoners sometimes falling while connected men continued moving, dragging them before the rest of the line could stop.

The economic arrangement behind that scene explains why the system expanded. Private operators gained access to a workforce whose movement and labor could be compelled under criminal sentences, while the state reduced some of the direct expense of maintaining prisoners. Florida archival materials describe incarcerated people remaining wards of the state while private firms used their labor in industries that included railroads, timber, agriculture, and turpentine.

The People Powell Remembered

The American Siberia is crowded with names. Powell remembered administrators, guards, cooks, escapees, injured prisoners, people who resisted labor, people who became trusted workers, and people whose lives ended in custody. He frequently introduces them at moments of crisis, which gives the memoir its remarkable energy but also means that his portraits are shaped by the perspective of the man responsible for controlling them.

The surviving records allow some of these people to be approached from another direction. Census schedules give occupations and ages. Prison registers add counties, offenses, sentence lengths, reception dates, and dispositions. The strongest identifications occur when several of those fields converge instead of relying only on a similar sounding name.

Noble Hawkins and Hunger at Lake Eustis

Powell places Noble Hawkins inside one of the most disturbing episodes in the book. During railroad work near Lake Eustis, Powell remembered food supplies deteriorating until prisoners searched the surrounding landscape for something they could eat. He claimed that Hawkins survived for fourteen days on palmetto tops and a small amount of salt.

The state prison register provides an unusually strong independent record for a man with that name. It records Noble Hawkins as a twenty one year old Black man born in Georgia who was serving a ten year sentence. Powell also remembered a ten year sentence, although he identified Hawkins with Nassau County while the surviving register gives Duval County.

That disagreement should remain visible. The register strengthens the argument that Powell was remembering a real person in the Florida prison population, but it does not verify the starvation story simply because the name matches. Powell remains the source for the fourteen day account.

The distinction makes Hawkins one of the best examples of how this project should work. The archive can independently recover the person without automatically authenticating every detail of the story told about him.

Sol Love and a Break for Freedom

Sol Love appears in a very different kind of story. Powell describes a small group of prisoners working near Live Oak under a guard he identifies as Hurst. During hot weather, Love reportedly helped distract the guard while other prisoners began running, then fled in another direction himself.

Powell says the escape triggered a pursuit involving Major Wise, himself, and a guard he calls McIntyre. Peter Reddick and George Gomez were captured near Sanderson, while a man believed to be Love disappeared into swamp country after shots were fired. Powell later reported hearing that a wounded stranger reached the St. Marys area and died, and he connected that report to Love.

The story should remain exactly what the evidence presently allows it to be, an account written by Powell. No secure independent prison register or death record for Sol Love has yet been tied to the episode in the records reviewed for this project.

That uncertainty does not make the episode useless. The story still reveals how Powell described guard staffing, escape opportunities, the geography of pursuit, firearms, communication between communities, and the difficulty of establishing what happened to a fugitive after he disappeared from the immediate reach of the camp.

Columbus See and the Sing Sing Breakout

Columbus See gives Powell one of his most elaborate escape narratives. According to the memoir, See earned enough trust to work as a cook at a camp Powell called Sing Sing. Powell says See gradually obtained rifle cartridges, hiding them until he and other prisoners were prepared to act.

The breakout began with an attack on yard guard W. J. Hillman. Powell says John G. Lippford and John Williams joined See, weapons were taken, additional prisoners became armed, and the escapees eventually left the camp with pursuit dogs before crossing the Suwannee River. The account reads with the momentum of an adventure story, but the practical information beneath it is even more revealing.

A cook could obtain unusual access. Ammunition security could fail one cartridge at a time. A camp could become vulnerable when trusted assignments, guard routines, personal relationships, and a remote setting came together. The details tell us how fragile authority could become even in a system built around armed supervision.

The 1880 census also gives one portion of Powell's story an independent anchor. It records Winder J. Hillman, age twenty three, as a guard associated with the state prison operation. That combination of surname, initials, occupation, and period makes the correspondence with Powell's W. J. Hillman particularly strong.

Dick Evans and the Hounds

Richard "Dick" Evans is one of the strongest examples of Powell's memoir colliding with an official record. Powell described Evans as a former Pensacola lawman imprisoned for murder and serving a five year sentence. He later became a hospital steward, a position that appears to have given him somewhat greater movement and opportunity than an ordinary work assignment.

Powell says Evans prepared carefully for escape. He sawed through his restraint, arranged bedding so that his bunk appeared occupied, cut through the wall, and disappeared. Once the pursuit began, Evans reportedly attempted to defeat the hounds by doubling back toward his own trail, slipping aside, and allowing the dogs and mounted pursuers to pass over the route they had already followed.

The Florida State Prison Register records Richard Evans, a white Ohio born man convicted of murder in Escambia County and sentenced to five years. Those details correspond closely with Powell's description of a Pensacola area man serving five years for a killing. The name, county, offense, sentence, birthplace, and chronology together make Evans one of the most persuasive identifications yet found.

The register cannot tell us whether Evans made a dummy in his bed or attempted to fool the dogs. Those details still belong to Powell. What the register does establish is that a prisoner with an unusually close biographical profile existed in the Florida system at the correct time.

That is enough to change the experience of reading the chapter. Dick Evans is no longer only a colorful character in Powell's book. Richard Evans becomes a person who can be placed in a government record and then returned to the story with far greater confidence.

When Escape Became a Science

Powell devoted considerable attention to pursuit dogs. He described the differences between types of hounds, their speed, their ability to maintain a scent, the difficulty mounted riders had keeping pace, and the methods used to train animals for prisoner pursuit. Escape was treated as a technical problem that required its own equipment, experience, and strategy.

That makes the chase narratives more than entertainment. They expose another layer of prison administration. Camps had to anticipate where a person might run, how quickly guards could respond, which roads or rivers might be used, and how dogs would follow a trail through the Florida landscape.

The people running understood the same geography. Powell's stories repeatedly describe attempts to use water, swamps, old trails, doubling back, false directions, darkness, or distance to disrupt pursuit. The contest was therefore not simply guard against prisoner. It involved knowledge of the land.

Self Injury, Desperation, and the Refusal of Work

Not every attempt to escape labor involved running. Powell records several acts of severe self injury committed by men facing punishment or continued work. These passages are among the hardest in the memoir because Powell sometimes describes them with the detached tone of an administrator trying to explain behavior he considered irrational or troublesome.

One man Powell identifies as Watson was described as a Black preacher from Madison County imprisoned for stealing cotton. Powell says Watson cut his own throat after a guard threatened punishment for working too slowly. He survived the injury, according to Powell, after receiving emergency treatment.

James Peterson appears in another episode connected directly to turpentine labor. Powell says Peterson drove an axe into his own foot in an attempt to avoid returning to the work. He later injured the foot again, and Powell reports that infection followed and Peterson eventually died.

These stories should not all be reduced to suicide. The surviving narrative does not always establish whether the person wanted to die, wanted to disable himself temporarily, hoped to escape work, acted under panic, or combined several motives. Powell gives us the event from the administrator's perspective, but the inner decision of the incarcerated person is largely inaccessible.

That silence matters. A person willing to cut his throat, damage his eyesight, or drive an axe through his own foot raises a question about the choices available inside the labor system. The memoir cannot answer every part of that question, but it makes the question impossible to ignore.

No. 47 and a Story Powell Heard Secondhand

Some of the darkest material in The American Siberia comes from events Powell admits he did not personally witness. One involves a prisoner known only as No. 47, a man Powell says repeatedly attempted escape. After one plan failed, Powell claims officials deliberately placed the man in a blacksmith shop near an open window while a guard waited outside.

In Powell's version, No. 47 climbed through the opening and the waiting guard shot him. Powell attributes the story to a former deputy warden rather than presenting himself as an eyewitness, which changes how the episode should be presented on the website.

The allegation is serious, but the present record does not independently establish the prisoner's name, the date, the guard, an official investigation, or the legal explanation for the shooting. Until a prison record, newspaper account, coroner's document, or other contemporary source can be connected to the event, it should remain identified as a killing described in Powell's memoir.

The fact that Powell preserved only a number makes the story even more unsettling. Other people in the book survive with names and fragments of biography. No. 47 survives primarily as an administrative identity attached to an alleged death.

Disease and Death in the Camps

Violent encounters were not the only way prisoners died. Powell repeatedly describes sickness moving through camps already weakened by difficult labor, primitive housing, exposure, poor sanitation, and unreliable food. Dysentery, fever, pneumonia, measles, infected wounds, and other illnesses appear throughout the memoir.

Powell also acknowledges that mortality could be difficult to reconstruct. In some passages he admits that accurate counts were not available to him. That admission is important because the system could maintain information necessary to organize labor while still leaving later researchers uncertain about everyone who died within it.

The deaths of staff also appear in the narrative. Powell says Jason Moore and guard William Rich died during a measles outbreak, demonstrating that disease did not stop at the line separating guards from prisoners. Remote camps with limited medical infrastructure exposed everyone present, although incarceration meant that prisoners had far less control over whether they could leave, rest, seek treatment, or refuse work.

The website should therefore avoid presenting death only through sensational episodes. A man shot during escape may be easier to narrate than a man who died after an infected wound or weeks of illness, but both belong to the history of the institution.

The Guards Become Names

The 1880 census is especially valuable because it turns Powell's general references to guards into identifiable people. The transcription records a surprisingly young staff, with many men in their early twenties. Hardy Hay was nineteen, Abner Oliver and John Clinton were twenty, and several others were only a few years older.

That age distribution changes how the camps can be imagined. Authority rested partly in the hands of young men carrying firearms and supervising prisoners in isolated work environments where immediate oversight from state officials could be limited. Their youth does not excuse conduct or establish that they behaved irresponsibly, but it adds important human context to the institution Powell describes.

Young People Inside the 1880 Prison Labor Population

The census transcription also records people younger than eighteen among the populations identified as prisoners or turpentine workers. Seven people in those categories were under eighteen, and all seven were recorded as Black. Five carried the occupational designation "Turpentine," while two were explicitly identified as "Prisoner."

The distinction between those census labels should be preserved because an occupation field does not, by itself, reconstruct the entire legal status of a person. The records nevertheless establish that adolescents were present inside the population associated with Florida's state prison labor operation in 1880.

Cy Williams and the Problem of Memory

Powell's recollection of Cy Williams produces one of the most complicated archival questions in the book. Powell describes Williams as receiving a twenty year sentence for horse stealing and associates him with the earliest period of Florida's prison system. He also gives him a specific place in the institutional story and describes repetitive brick carrying under the earlier Chattahoochee administration.

The surviving records do not reproduce that account neatly. Alachua County materials preserve forms including Sy Wilson, Sy Willson, and Cyrus Wilson alias Cyrus Williams. A prison record contains Si Williams, while the 1880 census transcription contains Sy Williams. The explicit use of "Cyrus Wilson alias Cyrus Williams" makes the Wilson and Williams connection especially interesting, but it does not prove that every similar spelling belongs to the man Powell remembered.

Nineteenth century names frequently changed in the hands of clerks and enumerators. A person might use an alias, a clerk could write a name phonetically, handwriting could later be misread, and familiar names could appear in shortened forms. Richard and Dick provide an easy example of an obvious variation, while Cy, Si, Sy, Cyrus, Wilson, Willson, and Williams require considerably greater caution.

The better approach is cumulative. A name becomes more persuasive when county, offense, sentence, age, birthplace, date, and institutional setting also agree. The website should therefore display the records beside one another rather than quietly collapsing them into one biography.

Florida Was Changing While Powell Was Watching

Powell's stories become more meaningful when placed against the larger transformation occurring in Florida. The state was emerging from Reconstruction, rebuilding transportation networks, expanding commercial agriculture, developing the pine and naval stores industries, and experimenting with ways to make imprisonment less expensive to government.

The timing matters. Malachi Martin's Chattahoochee administration belonged to the first phase of centralized state imprisonment after the Civil War. Powell's arrival in the mid 1870s came as Florida moved more aggressively toward private contracting and dispersed labor. The 1880 census captures the system after that transition had advanced far enough to place a contractor, Powell, guards, turpentine workers, and prisoners inside the same documentary frame.

What Powell Saw and What the Archive Adds

The American Siberia remains powerful because Powell gives the system movement. He describes trains arriving, chains being inspected, men disappearing into woods, hounds being released, resin being dipped from pine trees, guards losing control, prisoners injuring themselves, sickness spreading through camps, and desperate people attempting to turn Florida's geography into a route toward freedom.

The archive adds a different kind of detail. It gives John C. Powell an age and occupation. It turns Major Wise into a probable Henry A. Wyse, contractor of the state prison. It gives W. J. Hillman a probable first name. It places Richard Evans in Escambia County with a murder conviction and five year sentence. It records Noble Hawkins with an age, birthplace, conviction, and sentence.

That difference is why neither source should replace the other. Powell can tell us how he remembered an escape, but a register may tell us what eventually happened to the person. A census can establish that a guard existed at the right place and time, but it cannot tell us what he did during a confrontation years before the census taker arrived.

The most revealing moments occur when the records disagree. Noble Hawkins produces a county discrepancy. The Williams material produces changing names and conflicting administrative details. Sol Love remains elusive. No. 47 remains unnamed. Those problems are not failures of the article because they show the limits of what can still be recovered.

The records also expose the limitations of Powell himself. He wrote years after many of the events and shaped his narrative for readers. He sometimes treated manhunts like adventures, categorized prisoners through stereotypes, and distinguished the punishments he considered excessive from disciplinary violence he regarded as legitimate administration. His proximity to the camps made his testimony valuable, but it also means that his memoir preserves the perspective of a man who exercised authority within the system he described.

The American Siberia After the Last Page

Powell published his book in 1891, but the system he described did not disappear with the final chapter. Florida continued using leased prison labor well into the twentieth century, and the industries, contractors, roads, forests, and work camps associated with compulsory labor remained part of the state's penal landscape long after Powell's own years as a captain had ended.

The later history would bring more regulation, more state inspection, prison farms, road labor, and eventually a more centralized correctional bureaucracy. Yet Powell's memoir captures an earlier moment when the boundaries of imprisonment were unusually fluid. A prison could be a log sleeping house in the woods. A labor contract could determine where a person lived. A pine tree could become part of a sentence.

That is what makes The American Siberia worth returning to more than a century later. Its pages are filled with stories that initially seem almost impossible to separate from nineteenth century storytelling, but names in the census and prison registers repeatedly bring the narrative back to identifiable people. Some emerge clearly. Others appear only briefly before disappearing again.

The deeper the records are compared, the less Powell's Florida looks like an exotic collection of old prison tales. It becomes a populated historical landscape of contractors, captains, guards, cooks, laborers, prisoners, adolescents, fugitives, sick men, injured men, and people whose final fate is still uncertain.

Powell called it an American Siberia. The surviving records allow readers to look past the title and begin meeting the people who were actually there.

Sources and Further Reading

J. C. Powell, The American Siberia, or Fourteen Years' Experience in a Southern Convict Camp, 1891. This memoir provides the principal narrative source for Powell's career, camp organization, turpentine work, punishment, escapes, guards, prisoners, illnesses, self injury, and deaths discussed throughout the article. The opening framing of the project follows the book's identification of Powell as a Florida convict camp captain and the article's emphasis on the pine forests, labor camps, and surviving records.

Florida State Prison Register, Florida State Archives and Florida Memory. The register is especially important for comparing Powell's recollections with administrative information concerning names, ages, counties, offenses, sentences, dates of reception, and dispositions.

1880 United States Census, state prison staff, turpentine workers, and prisoners. The transcription used in this project provides the named staff list, including Henry A. Wyse, John C. Powell, Winder J. Hillman, Jacen N. Moore, Daniel T. Mc Intire, and other personnel, as well as the population categorized as prisoners and turpentine workers.

Alachua County Clerk Historical Records. These records preserve the Sy Wilson, Sy Willson, and Cyrus Wilson alias Cyrus Williams entries relevant to the Williams name investigation. The name cluster should remain an open identification problem rather than being represented as a settled biography.

Mildred L. Fryman, "Career of a 'Carpetbagger': Malachi Martin in Florida." Fryman's research is particularly useful for reconstructing Martin's military career, prison administration, political activity, agricultural interests, and the places where Powell's later recollections require qualification.

Jeffrey A. Drobney, "Where Palm and Pine Are Blowing: Convict Labor in the North Florida Turpentine Industry, 1877-1923," Florida Historical Quarterly 72, no. 4. Drobney provides the broader economic and labor history necessary to understand the turpentine operations that appear repeatedly in Powell's memoir.

Florida Memory and the State Archives of Florida. Their prison, convict lease, photographic, and administrative collections provide essential context for Florida's transition from Chattahoochee to leased labor and for the industries in which incarcerated people were employed.

University of Florida and United States Forest Service materials on naval stores and Florida forestry are useful for explaining the physical process behind boxing, chipping, dipping, collecting resin, distillation, turpentine production, and rosin production. These sources should accompany Powell's descriptions so general readers understand what the work actually required.
