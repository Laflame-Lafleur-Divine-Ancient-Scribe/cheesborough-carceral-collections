Fortune Ferguson / Newberry and Alachua County

Fortune Ferguson and the Newberry Six

This page concerns two Newberry-connected family histories: Fortune Ferguson's family and community record, and the families of the six Black people killed in the 1916 violence remembered as the Newberry Six.

The connection is Newberry, not Powell

Fortune Ferguson's family history and the Newberry Six belong together here because the records place both stories in the Newberry and Alachua County region. J. C. Powell's The American Siberia is not part of this argument and is kept on a separate Florida prison-history page. No source displayed here proves that Fortune was related to any Newberry Six victim; the connection under investigation is geographic and familial context, not an assumed genealogy.

Evidence boundary: Spelling similarities, shared county geography, and proximity in time do not prove kinship. A Ferguson-Newberry family relationship requires repeated census matches, addresses, court records, directories, family testimony, or another direct source.

Fortune's family and community record

The supplied census and archival materials are leads for reconstructing Fortune's family world: names, ages, households, occupations, places, and the records created before and after his prosecution. The spelling Fergson appears in some transcriptions; that variation must be preserved and tested rather than silently normalized to Ferguson.

Fortune's case also raises questions about how relatives and neighbors were represented by the state and press. Which family members appear in census schedules? Who could speak for him? What did prison and execution records omit? A family history should follow those people through records without turning them into background characters in Fortune's prosecution.

The Newberry Six: six people and their families

On August 19, 1916, after the shooting and death of Constable George Wynne near Jonesville and Newberry, a mob killed six Black people. The JMU marker record formally identifies five people taken from the Newberry jail and hanged: Gilbert Dennis, Mary Dennis, Stella Young, Andrew McHenry, and Reverend Joshua Baskin. James Dennis was also shot by the mob, which is why descendants and community memorials use “Newberry Six.”

The family relationships are essential. Gilbert and Mary Dennis were siblings; Mary was described as pregnant and the mother of four. Stella Young was reported as Boisey Long's partner and the mother of his son; Andrew McHenry was her brother. Joshua Baskin was a farmer and pastor. These were families, workers, church members, and neighbors, not merely six names in a marker database.

The page asks whether Fortune's family can be located within the same Newberry social world, not whether two tragedies can be made into one story.

Trauma and the limits of diagnosis

It is reasonable to investigate whether Fortune experienced trauma, mental illness, developmental disability, or other conditions that affected how authorities treated him. It is not responsible to diagnose him from a census entry, execution notice, or retrospective account. The relevant evidence would include contemporaneous medical examinations, competency findings, prison medical files, family testimony, and the exact language used by officials.
