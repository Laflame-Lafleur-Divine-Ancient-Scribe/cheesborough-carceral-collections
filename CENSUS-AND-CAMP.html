Newberry / Alachua County / 1916-1927

The Newberry Six, Fortune Ferguson, and the Political Climate of Alachua County, Florida

In August 1916, an attempted arrest near Newberry ended with the deaths of at least six Black residents. Eleven years later, the state of Florida executed Fortune Ferguson Jr. in its new electric chair. This page reads the two events as one political history of local power and the law.

How this page works: The Newberry killings, the prosecution and execution of Boisey Long, and the case of Fortune Ferguson Jr. are presented here as connected episodes in the political history of Alachua County between 1916 and 1927.

The Newberry killings, August 1916

In August 1916, the political order of Alachua County was exposed through a sequence of events that began with an attempted arrest in the small agricultural community of Newberry and ended with the deaths of at least six Black residents. The immediate dispute concerned Boisey Long, a Black farmer accused of stealing hogs. On the evening of August 17, Constable George Wynne and Dr. L. G. Harris went to Long's home in an effort to arrest him. Accounts of the confrontation differ over who fired first, but Wynne was killed, Harris was wounded, and Long fled into the surrounding countryside. What followed was not simply a search for an accused man. During the next two days, local authorities and armed white residents expanded the search to people believed to be associated with Long, and the distinction between official law enforcement and private violence became increasingly difficult to maintain. James Dennis was shot and killed during the search. Bert Dennis, Mary Dennis, Stella Young, Andrew McHenry, and Reverend Joshua Baskin were taken into custody and subsequently removed from the Newberry jail by a white mob. They were carried to a location outside town and hanged. Contemporary accounts and later historical investigations have suggested that the number of Black people killed during the violence may have exceeded the six traditionally identified as the Newberry Six. Alachua County's later compilation of reported lynchings also records additional deaths associated with the episode.

Government was present throughout the episode

The significance of the Newberry killings lies partly in the fact that they occurred within a county that possessed an established system of courts, sheriffs, constables, jails, and elected officials. The violence therefore cannot be understood simply as an eruption of disorder in a place where government was absent. Local government was present throughout the episode. Authorities attempted to arrest Long, organized a posse, detained people suspected of assisting him, and maintained prisoners in the county's custody. The mob's seizure of those prisoners represented a failure of the state's immediate control over its own jail, but it also occurred within a political environment in which white citizens understood that violence against Black residents could be carried out with little expectation of punishment. No one was held accountable for the murders of the Newberry victims. Long, however, was captured, prosecuted, convicted by an all white jury, sentenced to death, and executed in the yard of the Alachua County jail on October 27, 1916. The contrast between those two outcomes would remain important for understanding the political climate of Alachua County during the decade that followed.

A county familiar with racial violence

Alachua County was not unfamiliar with racial violence before the events at Newberry. The county's later historical research has documented numerous lynchings of Black residents during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, with Newberry becoming one of the principal locations of such violence. County records now identify dozens of reported lynchings across Alachua County, although the precise number remains subject to continuing research because many killings were poorly documented or disappeared from official records altogether. Newberry itself experienced repeated episodes of racial violence, including the killings of 1916 and the later lynching of Abraham Wilson in 1923. The recurrence of such violence provides a necessary political context for the Newberry Six. The killings did not introduce racial terror into Alachua County. They occurred within an existing system of white political control in which Black residents possessed limited protection from the institutions that governed their communities.

Political power and the meaning of justice

By 1916, the political order of Alachua County rested upon more than the formal separation of Black and white life associated with Jim Crow. It also depended upon the practical distribution of political power. White officials controlled the principal institutions of local government, while Black residents faced the broader consequences of disfranchisement and exclusion that had spread throughout Florida after Reconstruction. Under those conditions, the meaning of criminal justice could vary according to the race of the person accused and the race of the person harmed. The state could act decisively when white authority appeared to have been challenged, particularly when the alleged victim was a white officer, while demonstrating considerably less determination when Black residents became victims of violence committed by white citizens. The Newberry killings exposed this imbalance because the same local political system that pursued Long with extraordinary speed did not produce comparable accountability for the men who removed Black prisoners from official custody and killed them.

The prosecution and execution of Boisey Long

The sequence is particularly revealing because Long did not remain outside the reach of government for long. He surrendered to authorities two days after Wynne was killed. Local officials understood that leaving him in Alachua County presented a danger because of the possibility that a mob would kill him before he could be brought to trial. He was therefore removed from the immediate area and eventually returned for prosecution. On September 7, 1916, an all white jury convicted him after approximately seven minutes of deliberation. He was sentenced to death and executed less than two months after the confrontation with Wynne. The speed of the proceedings contrasted sharply with the absence of comparable legal consequences for the people responsible for the Newberry killings.

Law and mob violence could coexist

This difference should not be reduced to a simple claim that one proceeding was legal and the other was illegal. The distinction between lawful punishment and lynching remained fundamental, but the political history becomes clearer when the two events are placed within the same institutional setting. The county possessed the capacity to identify a Black defendant, protect him from a mob, prosecute him, secure a conviction, and carry out a death sentence. What the Newberry episode demonstrated was that the existence of that capacity did not guarantee that it would be applied equally. The law could protect a defendant from one form of violence while simultaneously failing to protect other Black residents from the violence of the community itself.

The Newberry Six therefore reveal an important feature of racial politics in Alachua County. White political authority did not depend upon choosing between formal law and mob violence. The two could coexist. Local officials could attempt to maintain order while members of the white community demanded a more immediate form of racial punishment. The state could condemn disorder when disorder threatened its own authority while failing to impose meaningful consequences upon those who exercised violence against Black residents. In this setting, the problem was not simply that a mob temporarily defeated the law. The larger problem was that the boundaries between the political community, local government, and racial violence were sufficiently porous that the failure to prosecute the mob did not destroy the legitimacy of the existing political order.

Florida centralizes the death penalty

The years after 1916 brought changes to Florida's criminal justice system that altered the manner in which the state exercised punishment. The transformation was especially significant in the administration of capital punishment. Before the 1920s, Florida's death penalty remained substantially decentralized. Counties carried much of the responsibility for confinement, transportation, and execution, and local officials occupied a central place in the final stages of a capital case. The legislature's creation of a centralized system in the 1920s changed that arrangement. Florida established a permanent death chamber at the state prison and adopted electrocution as the method of execution. The reform formed part of a broader effort to remove important criminal justice functions from the decentralized system that had previously given considerable authority to local communities, officials, and private actors.

The change in the machinery of punishment did not occur independently of the political history of Florida's counties. The move toward centralization emerged after years in which local control had produced serious problems for the state's criminal justice system. Local officials remained important, but their responsibilities increasingly operated within a statewide structure. The new system did not eliminate the sheriff or county government from capital cases. Instead, it placed their actions within a process controlled increasingly by state institutions. The case of Fortune Ferguson Jr. provides an unusually clear view of that transition because his prosecution began in Alachua County and became one of the earliest cases to pass through Florida's new system of state administered electrocution.

Fortune Ferguson Jr. and the new machinery of execution

Fortune Ferguson was a Black teenager from Alachua County who was accused of raping an eight year old white girl in 1924. He was sixteen years old at the time of the offense, according to Brandon T. Jett's recent study of the case, and he became the first person sentenced to die in Florida's newly created electric chair. His case was therefore not simply another capital prosecution. It occurred at the moment when Florida was attempting to redefine who possessed authority over the death penalty and how that authority would be exercised. The state had created a new mechanism for carrying out executions, but the case still depended upon the participation of local officials.

The initial prosecution remained deeply connected to Alachua County. Ferguson was arrested, prosecuted, convicted, and sentenced through the local criminal justice system before the state assumed responsibility for the final stages of his punishment. The judgment ordered that he be delivered by the sheriff of Alachua County to the proper officer of the state penitentiary and held there until the governor issued a warrant fixing the time of execution. The language of the judgment illustrates the institutional transition taking place in Florida. The county sheriff remained responsible for transferring the condemned prisoner, but the execution itself belonged to the state and was to occur within the permanent death chamber created by state law.

The surviving administrative record makes the relationship between county and state authority even clearer. In 1926, the Florida Attorney General's Office recorded that a death warrant had been issued for Ferguson and that the Alachua County sheriff had conveyed him to the state prison farm for execution. A court order subsequently stayed the execution, and the Attorney General's Office concluded that Ferguson's custody therefore reverted to the Alachua County sheriff until another warrant could be issued. The county remained responsible for the costs associated with his transportation and confinement while the state controlled the legal process governing the execution.

A cooperative system of county and state authority

The arrangement matters because it demonstrates that Florida's new centralized criminal justice system did not simply replace local authority with state authority. Local and state officials worked together. The sheriff remained an essential actor, the county remained responsible for certain expenses and custody decisions, and state officials controlled the death warrant and the execution itself. The transition therefore strengthened formal governmental authority at both levels. As Jett argues, Ferguson's case demonstrates that centralization was not simply a struggle between state officials and local officials. It produced a cooperative system in which state and local actors could reinforce one another's authority.

The political significance of Ferguson's case becomes clearer when it is placed alongside the Newberry Six. In 1916, local officials had struggled to maintain control over Black prisoners after a white mob mobilized against them. Eleven years later, the state had developed a system through which it could carry out a death sentence without relying upon a county jail, a local execution yard, or the physical presence of a public crowd. The method had changed from hanging to electrocution, and the location had changed from the county to the state prison. The death penalty had become more centralized, more bureaucratic, and more insulated from the direct intervention of local crowds.

The transformation did not eliminate the significance of race

Yet the transformation did not eliminate the political significance of race.

The Newberry Six were killed by white citizens outside the formal criminal process, while Ferguson was executed through a formal criminal process controlled increasingly by the state. The two cases cannot be treated as legally equivalent. One involved lynching, the other a judicial sentence. One involved prisoners seized from a local jail, the other a defendant whose custody was transferred through official channels. One represented the failure of the state to prevent a mob from exercising lethal power, while the other represented the state's growing ability to exercise lethal power itself.

The historical connection lies elsewhere. Both cases demonstrate how the authority of the state operated in relation to Black life in Alachua County. In 1916, the state failed to impose meaningful punishment upon white residents who killed Black people during the Newberry violence, while moving rapidly to prosecute the Black man accused of killing a white officer. In Ferguson's case, the state exercised its own authority over the life of a Black teenager through a formal capital punishment system. The political environment had therefore changed in important institutional respects, but the racial distribution of vulnerability remained a central feature of the criminal justice system.

The limits of administrative punishment

The difference between the two episodes also reveals how the development of a modern criminal justice system can obscure the political conditions that preceded it. The electric chair presented punishment as an administrative procedure. A condemned prisoner was transferred, a warrant was issued, a date was established, and prison officials carried out the sentence. The process appeared fundamentally different from the public violence of a lynching. It was conducted behind prison walls and according to written rules. Its authority rested upon statutes, judicial decisions, executive warrants, and administrative procedures rather than upon the immediate presence of an armed crowd.

That difference was real. It was also incomplete.

The state's increasing control over executions did not mean that the political assumptions underlying criminal prosecution had been transformed at the same speed. The centralized system inherited defendants, prosecutors, sheriffs, courts, and communities from the earlier system. It therefore carried forward many of the racial relationships that had shaped local criminal justice before centralization. The state could remove the physical execution from the county without removing the county's political influence over who entered the capital punishment system in the first place.

This is particularly important in Ferguson's case because his prosecution originated within Alachua County. The state did not independently identify Ferguson as a capital defendant and impose punishment upon him from a distant administrative center. The case emerged from the local criminal justice system and then traveled through the newly centralized state apparatus. Local accusation and prosecution preceded state execution. The county and the state therefore formed a continuous institutional chain rather than two separate systems.

One history, two mechanisms of death

The Newberry Six and Fortune Ferguson consequently belong to the same history even though their deaths occurred through profoundly different mechanisms. The first case reveals the weakness of formal authority when white residents mobilized to enforce racial power outside the courtroom. The second reveals the growing strength of formal authority when the state consolidated control over capital punishment. Between the two stood a period of institutional change in which Florida's government attempted to establish a more reliable monopoly over criminal punishment.

The political climate of Alachua County remained central to that development because the county was not merely a geographic location in which these cases occurred. It was the political environment through which the defendants, victims, officials, and institutions interacted. Newberry's racial history, Gainesville's political institutions, the county sheriff's office, local courts, and the communities surrounding them all influenced how criminal justice operated. The state could centralize the execution chamber, but it could not separate that chamber entirely from the local political structures that produced capital cases.

The persistence of lynching

The later history of Newberry reinforces the point. In January 1923, only a few years after the 1916 killings, a Black prisoner named Abraham Wilson was removed from jail and lynched in Newberry after being convicted of cattle stealing and sentenced to imprisonment. The killing occurred in the same broader landscape that had witnessed the Newberry Six. The continuity is significant because it demonstrates that the development of state controlled executions did not immediately end local racial violence. The centralized death chamber and the persistence of lynching existed within the same state during the same period.

Florida's political transformation therefore proceeded unevenly. The state could strengthen its institutional control over punishment while local communities continued to exercise racial power through extralegal violence. Centralization did not produce a clean transition from one system to another. For a period, the two systems existed simultaneously. The state increasingly claimed exclusive authority over legal executions while white mobs continued to demonstrate that racial violence remained possible outside the state's formal procedures.

April 27, 1927

Fortune Ferguson's execution in April 1927 represented an important moment in that transition. He was executed at the state prison on April 27, 1927, after years of litigation and administrative proceedings surrounding his conviction and death sentence. The Florida State Prison register records Ferguson as a Black prisoner from Alachua County who had been sentenced to death for rape and notes that a temporary reprieve had interrupted the original execution process before he was ultimately electrocuted.

His death marked the practical establishment of the system Florida had created several years earlier. The electric chair did not merely provide a new method of killing condemned prisoners. It relocated the state's ultimate punitive authority. The death sentence no longer ended at the county jail. It moved through a network of courts, sheriffs, executive warrants, state prison officials, and administrative procedures before reaching the death chamber.

The significance of that change becomes clearer when measured against what had happened in Newberry eleven years earlier. In 1916, a mob had taken prisoners from a county jail and asserted its own authority over their lives. In 1927, the state itself possessed the machinery necessary to determine the place, timing, and method of a prisoner's death. The government had strengthened its monopoly over punishment.

Modernization without equality

For Black residents of Alachua County, however, the development did not necessarily mean that the underlying political relationship with criminal justice had become more equitable. The Newberry Six had demonstrated that Black people could be killed by white citizens without meaningful legal accountability. Ferguson's case demonstrated that Black defendants could also be subjected to the most severe punishment available through the formal institutions of the state. The two forms of violence occupied different legal categories, but both must be understood within the racial politics of the period.

The historical record therefore complicates any interpretation that treats the modernization of Florida's criminal justice system as an uncomplicated expansion of justice. The system became more centralized and administratively coherent. State officials acquired greater control over executions. Courts and executive officers became more deeply integrated into the punishment process. Yet those institutional developments occurred within a political order that continued to distinguish sharply between Black defendants and white communities, between the protection afforded to white authority and the protection afforded to Black victims, and between the state's willingness to punish racialized offenses and its willingness to punish racial violence committed by whites.

By 1927, the machinery through which Florida administered capital punishment had changed substantially from the system that existed when the Newberry Six were murdered in 1916. Executions had become centralized at the state prison, appellate proceedings and executive warrants had become integral components of the process, and local officials increasingly operated within a statewide administrative structure rather than exercising authority over executions entirely within their own communities. Yet the transformation did not erase the political and racial conditions that had shaped criminal justice in Alachua County during the preceding decade. The same county that had failed to bring the perpetrators of the 1916 Newberry killings to justice had subsequently prosecuted Fortune Ferguson, a Black teenager, and participated in the bureaucratic process that ultimately delivered him to Florida's electric chair.

Conclusion

The significance of the Newberry Six and Fortune Ferguson therefore rests in the relationship between local political power and the development of state criminal justice. The Newberry killings reveal a political order in which racial violence could operate alongside local government and escape meaningful punishment. Ferguson's execution reveals a later political order in which the state had become sufficiently centralized to claim the administration of death as its own responsibility. Between those moments, Florida did not abandon the racial politics of punishment. It reorganized them.

The history of Alachua County during these years is consequently a history of changing institutions operating within persistent political conditions. The mob did not become the state, and the electric chair was not simply a continuation of the lynching tree. The difference between them matters. But the distance between them should not conceal the political environment through which both became possible. The Newberry Six belonged to a period in which white racial authority could be exercised through collective violence with little fear of prosecution. Fortune Ferguson belonged to a period in which that authority increasingly operated through centralized courts, prisons, warrants, and state administration.
