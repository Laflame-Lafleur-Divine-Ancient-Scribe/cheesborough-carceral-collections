Florida juvenile justice / case history

The Dunbar Village Case

Juvenile prosecution, adult punishment, victim-centered research, and the long legal history of a 2007 West Palm Beach crime.

The case and its legal afterlife

One prosecution, several legal histories

On June 18, 2007, a woman and her 12-year-old son were attacked inside their Dunbar Village apartment in West Palm Beach. Authorities alleged that multiple armed teenagers entered the home, sexually assaulted the woman, beat both victims, forced a sexual act involving the mother and child, and used cleaning chemicals in an attempt to destroy evidence.

The case became nationally visible because of the alleged violence. Its longer historical importance lies in what followed: three defendants were juveniles at the time of the offense but were prosecuted in adult court, received life sentences for non-homicide offenses, and later returned to court after the Supreme Court limited juvenile life-without-release punishment in Graham v. Florida.

Reading standard. This page separates allegations reported by authorities, convictions and sentences, later resentencing, and unresolved questions. The victims' experiences remain central; the later sentencing litigation does not erase the harm described in the trial record.

Procedural timeline

From arrest to resentencing

  1. June 18, 2007The attack occurred at Dunbar Village, according to contemporary reporting.
  2. July-August 2007Taylor, Walker, Lawson, and Poindexter were indicted in separate stages; the younger defendants were prosecuted as adults.
  3. August 2009Lawson pleaded guilty and cooperated. Walker and Poindexter were tried before separate juries.
  4. 2009Taylor, Walker, and Poindexter received convictions and severe sentences, including life sentences for the two juvenile defendants.
  5. 2010-2011Graham v. Florida changed the constitutional meaning of juvenile life sentences for non-homicide offenses; WPBF reported Taylor and Walker were resentenced to 60 years.
Evidence and responsibility

Group violence still requires individual proof

Contemporary coverage described fingerprints, DNA, victim testimony, and cooperating-witness testimony as parts of the prosecution evidence. In a multi-defendant case, the state may argue collective participation while juries must still decide each defendant's criminal responsibility under the charges and instructions before them.

Lawson's cooperation illustrates how plea bargaining can change a person's role in a case. He moved from defendant to prosecution witness, while the trial defendants challenged the reliability and incentives of his testimony. The distinction between shared conduct and individualized proof is one of the case's central research questions.
