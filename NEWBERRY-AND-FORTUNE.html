Alachua County, Florida / Racial Violence, Capital Punishment, and State Authority, 1916 to 1927

The Newberry Lynchings and Fortune Ferguson Jr.: Race, Capital Punishment, and the Transformation of State Authority in Florida

This archival study examines the 1916 Newberry lynchings, the prosecution and execution of Boisey Long, and the later capital case of Fortune Ferguson Jr. as distinct but historically related episodes within the development of criminal punishment in Alachua County and the State of Florida. Between Long's execution in Gainesville in 1916 and Ferguson's execution at Florida State Prison in 1927, Florida transferred the administration of capital punishment from county sheriffs to the state prison system and replaced hanging with electrocution. The records assembled here document that transformation while preserving the legal distinction between racial terror carried out outside judicial process and executions imposed through formal criminal proceedings.

I. Newberry and the Changing Administration of Death in Florida

The events that unfolded in western Alachua County during August 1916 began with an attempted arrest and developed into one of the most consequential episodes of racial violence in the region's history. Constable George Wynne and Dr. L. G. Harris went to the property of Boisey Long near Newberry and Jonesville in connection with an accusation involving stolen hogs. Gunfire followed. Wynne received fatal wounds, Harris was wounded, and Long fled. The search for Long rapidly expanded beyond an effort to locate a single suspect as armed white residents and law enforcement personnel moved through the surrounding Black farming communities.

At least six Black residents were killed during the violence associated with the manhunt. James Dennis was killed during the search. Bert Dennis, Mary Dennis, Stella Young, Andrew McHenry, and Reverend Joshua Baskin were detained and held in the Newberry jail before a mob removed them from custody and lynched them. Mary Dennis was pregnant. The detention of five victims before their deaths places the episode directly within the history of public authority because the violence did not occur entirely outside governmental structures. The victims had entered official custody and nevertheless received no effective protection from the mob that seized them.

The historical record surrounding Newberry has continued to develop. The six victims traditionally associated with the mass lynching remain central to the episode, but subsequent research and community remembrance projects have documented a broader history of racial terror in Newberry. In 2021, the Alachua County Community Remembrance Project and the Equal Justice Initiative dedicated a historical marker at Freddie Warmack Park commemorating nine documented victims of racial terror lynching associated with Newberry. The broader memorial record includes victims from other years and must not be conflated with the six people conventionally identified with the August 1916 killings.

II. The Newberry Lynchings of August 1916

On August 17, 1916, authorities attempted to arrest Boisey Long near Newberry. The resulting exchange of gunfire left Constable George Wynne mortally wounded and Dr. L. G. Harris injured. Long escaped, and the effort to capture him drew armed men from the surrounding region into western Alachua County. Contemporary reporting, later historical research, and descendant testimony document a climate in which the search for Long expanded into violence against Black residents suspected of assisting him or connected to him through family and community relationships.

James Dennis was killed on August 18 during the manhunt. Five additional Black residents were taken into custody: Bert Dennis, his pregnant wife Mary Dennis, Stella Young, Andrew McHenry, and Reverend Joshua Baskin. During the early hours of August 19, a white mob removed the five prisoners from the Newberry jail and carried them to a site outside town, where they were lynched. The killings demonstrated the inability or unwillingness of local institutions to protect Black prisoners who had already been placed under official control.

III. Boisey Long, Criminal Prosecution, and County Execution

Boisey Long escaped the immediate manhunt and subsequently entered official custody outside Alachua County. Authorities transferred him to Jacksonville because confinement in Gainesville could expose him to mob violence before trial. His removal from the county illustrates a contradiction in the administration of justice after the Newberry killings. Officials acted to preserve Long for prosecution while five Black prisoners previously held at Newberry had been taken from custody and killed.

Long was returned to Gainesville for trial in September 1916. An all-white jury convicted him of first-degree murder in the death of George Wynne after a proceeding that moved with extraordinary speed. Contemporary accounts report that the jury deliberated for approximately seven minutes before returning its verdict. Long received no recommendation of mercy and was sentenced to death.

On October 27, 1916, officials hanged Long at the Alachua County Jail. His execution occurred under Florida's older decentralized system of capital punishment, under which county authorities retained responsibility for carrying out death sentences. Florida successfully prosecuted Long for the death of a white law enforcement officer, while no comparable criminal prosecution produced accountability for the Black residents killed during the Newberry violence.

The Imbalance of Southern Justice

While the State of Florida expended maximum resources to convict and hang Boisey Long in sixty days, a subsequent coroner's inquest and grand jury concluded that the Newberry Six were killed "at the hands of persons unknown." No white person was ever arrested, charged, or prosecuted for the murders of James Dennis, Bert Dennis, Mary Dennis, Stella Young, Andrew McHenry, or Joshua Baskin.

IV. Fortune Ferguson Jr. and Florida's New System of Capital Punishment

Florida reorganized the administration of capital punishment in 1923. The legislature transferred executions from county authorities to the state prison system and adopted electrocution as the state's method of execution. A capital prosecution could still originate in a local courthouse, but the final stages increasingly involved statewide institutions, including the governor, appellate courts, the state prison warden, and the administrative machinery of Florida State Prison.

Ferguson was a Black teenager from the Newberry area who was sixteen when authorities accused him of raping a white child in 1924. Federal census schedules preserved within this collection document the Ferguson family in western Alachua County before the criminal prosecution, providing evidence of family residence, household composition, and agricultural life that predates the legal record by which Ferguson later became known.

Ferguson was prosecuted in the Circuit Court of Alachua County and sentenced to death. Legal historian Brandon T. Jett identifies Ferguson as the first person sentenced to die in Florida's newly created electric chair. His case occurred while Florida was establishing a centralized system in which local courts and law enforcement remained important participants even as state institutions assumed responsibility for imprisonment, appellate administration, executive action, and execution.

Ferguson did not proceed immediately from sentence to execution. His case remained active for approximately three years as lawyers pursued appellate remedies and executive reprieves delayed the sentence. The surviving legal and prison records document interactions among the Alachua County sheriff, the courts, the governor, and state prison officials. On April 27, 1927, Florida executed Ferguson in the electric chair at Florida State Prison. He was eighteen years old.

V. From County Execution to State Administration

The institutional significance of Ferguson's case lies in the changing distribution of authority within Florida's criminal justice system. Before 1923, execution remained closely associated with county government. Sheriffs maintained custody of condemned prisoners and carried out hangings within their jurisdictions. The system tied capital punishment to the courthouse, jail, sheriff, and local political community in which the prosecution originated.

Centralization altered that arrangement without removing local officials from the process. Jett's study of Ferguson argues that Florida's new system developed through cooperation between state and local criminal justice officials. County authorities continued to investigate offenses, arrest defendants, assemble prosecutions, participate in trials, and exercise custodial responsibilities. State institutions increasingly governed imprisonment, appellate review, executive warrants, reprieves, and execution.

The Newberry lynchings and Ferguson's execution require a precise comparison. The 1916 lynchings were extrajudicial killings without judicial process. Ferguson's death followed indictment, trial, sentence, appellate litigation, executive action, and formal execution by the State of Florida. The legal distinction is fundamental. The episodes nevertheless belong to the same regional history of race, local authority, criminal accusation, state power, and capital punishment.

The comparison does not establish that Ferguson's prosecution resulted from the 1916 lynchings or that state execution represented a direct institutional continuation of mob violence. The surviving evidence supports a narrower comparison. Both histories developed within Alachua County while Florida was redefining the relationship between local criminal justice institutions and centralized state authority.

VII. Historical Memory and Public Commemoration

Public understanding of the Newberry killings developed gradually after the events themselves. Family memory, local historical research, journalism, oral history, community organizations, and archival work preserved information that did not occupy a prominent place in conventional accounts of Alachua County history.

1. Alachua County Soil Collection Project

The Alachua County Community Remembrance Project worked with the Equal Justice Initiative on historical research, public education, oral history, soil collection, and memorial projects. Soil gathered from sites associated with lynching preserves a material connection to the landscape in which the killings occurred.

2. Newberry Historical Marker

In August 2021, the Alachua County Community Remembrance Project and Equal Justice Initiative dedicated a historical marker at Freddie Warmack Park commemorating nine documented victims of racial terror lynching associated with Newberry. The marker distinguishes the broader history of racial terror from the six victims traditionally identified with the August 1916 mass lynching.

3. Descendant Memory and Oral History

Descendant testimony remains significant because families maintained names, relationships, locations, and recollections across generations when official records provided incomplete accounts of the violence. Oral history and public commemoration operate alongside documentary research rather than replacing it.

4. Public History

Historical markers, soil collections, journalism, memorial services, and community education demonstrate how the history has been researched and commemorated long after the original events.

IX. Archival Foundation: The Victor L. Streib Papers

The primary-source foundation of the Fortune Ferguson research owes a substantial debt to the M. E. Grenander Department of Special Collections and Archives at the University at Albany, State University of New York. The department preserves the National Death Penalty Archive, including the Victor L. Streib Papers, an extensive research collection documenting capital punishment and the execution of juveniles and women in the United States.

The Streib Papers contain a dedicated case file identified by the archive as "Florida: Fortune Ferguson, executed 1927-04-27." The file forms part of Collection APAP-330 and preserves research material concerning Ferguson within a larger body of documentation assembled by legal scholar Victor L. Streib.

Carceral Collections acknowledges the M. E. Grenander Department of Special Collections and Archives for preserving and providing access to these materials. The preservation of the Streib Papers allows Ferguson's case to be examined as more than an execution date by placing it within the longer documentary history of juvenile capital punishment.

X. Research and Archival Use

The Newberry and Fortune Ferguson collection brings together sources created for different purposes and at different moments in time. Court judgments document judicial action. Prison records document state custody and administration. Census schedules preserve household and demographic information. Newspapers record contemporary reporting as well as the assumptions and limitations of their period. Oral histories and descendant accounts preserve experiences and family knowledge that official institutions did not always record.

The collection yields its historical significance through relationships among sources, including agreements, discrepancies, omissions, chronology, and institutional provenance. Researchers should consult original documents, identify the creators and purposes of each record, and distinguish contemporary evidence from later interpretation.
