Abstract

This article uses the case of Fortune Ferguson Jr. to explore the creation and effects of a centralized justice system in Florida in the 1920s. This involved a 16-year-old Black teenager accused, convicted, and executed for rape in Florida in the mid-1920s ; the first person sentenced to die in Florida's newly created electric chair. The shift to the electric chair reflected a larger move by the legislature to remove criminal justice processes from the decentralized system that had existed prior to 1923. Rather than framing centralization as a competition between state and local criminal justice actors, the article argues that the case provides insight into how state and local officials collaborated to augment the authority of formal criminal justice at both levels.

Fortune Ferguson Jr. ; the record

At 2:30 p.m. on 27 April 1927, the state of Florida carried out the death sentence for Fortune Ferguson Jr., described in Jett's article as an 18-year-old African American from Alachua, Florida at the time of execution. The Washington Post juvenile death penalty chronology held in this collection records his age at the time of the crime as 13. The discrepancy between these sources ; and others in the archival file that place Ferguson's age between 13 and 16 at the time of the offense ; is itself a significant research question. Precise ages are not always reliably recorded in early twentieth-century Florida court and prison records, and disputed age claims were relevant to the legal proceedings at the time.

Legal change and racial preservation

"As the case of Fortune Ferguson illustrates, local and state authorities demonstrated that the newly centralized criminal justice apparatus could continue to reinforce and perpetuate white supremacy in the Jim Crow South without the criticisms of the old system."

Jett's central argument is that criminal justice centralization ; often presented as a progressive reform ; functioned in practice as "preservation through transformation," a term drawn from legal scholar Reva Siegel. The shift from public hangings to private state-run electrocutions removed the most visible excesses of racialized punishment from public view while preserving its racial inequities in a more procedurally legitimate form.

The electric chair as modernization

Florida's legislature approved the electric chair in 1923. Proponents argued that electrocution was more humane and civilized than hanging. The article traces how this humanitarian justification served a secondary function: it consolidated state authority over an execution process previously dominated by county sheriffs, local mobs, and private contractors operating convict-lease arrangements. The execution of Fortune Ferguson became a demonstration that the new system could function ; and could function in a racially predictable way ; without the spectacle of lynching.

Convict leasing and the decentralized system

Before the 1920s reforms, Florida's criminal justice system was highly decentralized. Punishment was distributed across county courts, local sheriffs, convict-leasing camps run by private contractors, and extralegal violence by white mobs. By the late 1910s this system was under pressure not primarily because of its racial violence but because of its inefficiency, public visibility, and rampant fraud in the leasing system. Reform came, in Jett's account, not to dismantle racial hierarchy but to preserve it through more defensible mechanisms.

State oversight and humanitarian critique

The Ferguson case raises persistent questions about what oversight meant in practice. Florida courts, prosecutors, and the pardon board all had formal authority over the case. The article describes the pardon board's role and notes that none of these formal mechanisms produced a different outcome. This is significant for researchers asking how humanitarian language functions within punitive systems: the claim that a state execution is more humane than a lynching does not require demonstrating that the condemned received a fair process. It requires only that the process appear orderly.

Criminological implications

Jett's article engages, through its historical case, with several recurring debates in criminology and the sociology of punishment:

Racial disproportionality ; The execution statistics make the racial pattern of Florida punishment legible in quantitative terms. Qualitative analysis of the Ferguson record can examine how that disproportion was produced at the level of individual prosecutorial and judicial decisions.

Deterrence and public spectacle ; The shift to private executions removed punishment from public view. If deterrence depends on visible consequences, what purpose does a private state execution serve? The article suggests it serves a different function: demonstrating the authority of formal justice actors rather than warning community observers.

Age, culpability, and mitigation ; Ferguson's disputed age at the time of the offense places his case at the intersection of juvenile justice and capital punishment. Later Supreme Court cases, documented in the Washington Post chronology also held in this collection, would eventually prohibit capital punishment for crimes committed before age 16. The Ferguson case predates those rulings and illustrates how age was treated as a factual dispute rather than a limiting principle.
