Counting confinement

From Bethune's household to juvenile institutions: how census categories make people visible, sortable, and governable.

The institutional record is an argument

The 1910 District 0163 workbook identifies Mary M. Bethune as a Black widow and principal on Second Avenue in Daytona Beach. It also lists Anita Pinckney, a 19-year-old Black inmate, as a student in the same household record. That juxtaposition should not be flattened into a single institutional story. It may document an educational or residential setting, but the workbook alone does not explain the legal status implied by â€œinmate.â€

Later workbooks in the collection identify girls' industrial schools, juvenile detention, and other institutions. Their value is not simply numerical. The categories show how public authority translated children and adults into administrative types: inmate, student, worker, resident, prisoner, patient, or staff.

To count an institution is also to decide what kind of person can be counted inside it.

Separate from Dozier, connected by method

This page does not merge the institutional census with the Dozier census or with Powell's camp. The records belong to different places, periods, and questions. They can still be read through a common method: identify the institution's stated purpose, inspect its categories, preserve the record's uncertainty, and ask what the form leaves out.

The Bethune workbook is especially useful as a counterpoint because it places an educational leader, a student, and a racialized institutional category in one local record. That does not resolve the meaning of â€œinmateâ€; it makes the question impossible to ignore.
